## acidity constant
- larger value of Ka indicates greater tendency of HA donate a proton to water: strong acid

	- a smaller value of Ka indicates greater tendency of A- accept a proton from water, and a stronger base.

## buffers (hoorah)
- buffer solution is one that resists changes in pH when proportional small quantities of acid or base are added
	- quote word for word.
- acidic buffer: weak acid + conjugate base$$CH_{3}COOH(aq)+H_{2}O(l)\rightleftharpoons CH_{3}COO^-(aq)+H_{3}O^+(aq)$$
	- add HCl -> $[H_{3}O^+]$ increases, shift pos eqm LEFT
		- CH3COO- -> CH3COOH
		- \[H3O+] ~ same
	- add NaOH
		- reacts with \[H3O+], reducing \[H3O+], shift pos eqm RIGHT
		- Ch3COOH -> CH3COO-
- basic buffer: weak base + conjugate acid$$NH_{3}(aq)+H_{2}O(l)\rightleftharpoons NH^+_{4}+(aq)+OH^-(aq)$$
	- add HCl: 
		- H+ reacts with OH-, decreases \[OH-],
		- pos eqm RIGHT
		- NH3 -> NH4+
		- \[OH-] ~same
	- add NaOH: \[OH-] increase
		- pos eqm shifts right
		- \[OH-] ~same no noticeable change in pH
- conjugate pair mixed together to form buffer solutions

## ionic product for water, Kw
- constant called ionic product for water, Kw, is defined as: $$K_{w}=[H^+][OH^-]=1 \times 10^-14$$ at 298 K (25degC)
- called the **ionic product of water**
- water is considered neutral as $[H^+]=[OH^-]$
- calculating pH of a strong base
- $K_w$ can be used to calculate $[H^+]$ for solutions of strong bases.
- $K_w$ = $[H^+][OH^-]$ therefore $[H^+]=\frac{K_{w}}{{[OH^-]}}$

## titrations
### back titrations
- back titration can be helpful in these scenarios
	- substance reacts with measured excess of acid or base, and measure the remaining unreacted acid or base is titrated in a regular titration, to establish the amount of acid or base left over
- why use back titration?
	- a substance is insoluble so you can't make a solution from it
	- a substance reacts slowly with acid or base, so does not give a rapid change in pH at the equivalence point

## errors in titrations
- accuracy
	- how close your value is to the true value
- precision - how close your measurement of value, how small the range is
- random error vs systematic error
	- systematic error: error which consistently causes your results to be lower or higher than the true value. similar every time.
		- always affect the accuracy of your results
	- random error: error that causes random deviations in your data.
		- can never be eliminated - only minimised and averaging the results.
		- cause inconsistent results
		- results can be above or below the true value, and magnitude of the error can vary too.
- systematic error
	- problems with primary standard:
		- incorrect mass of substance measured out
		- not all substance transferred to flask.
		- incorrect use of volumetric flak / meniscus (so volume is wrong)
	- incorrect choice of indicator
	- consistent parallax error - not a great example
- random error
	- washing pipette with distilled water
		- inconsistent because it is only valid the first time.
	- washing burette with distilled water
		- consistently decreases concentration of standard solution
		- decreasing total moles of standard solution
		- requires more titre to react fully
		- thus increasing titre and thus we overestimate the concentration of titre
	- washing volumetric flask with acid/base (consistently increases )
	- inconsistent colour of end point
	- careless reading/checking of meniscus n burette or pipette
	- measurement errors of equipment.
## random measurement errors
- hard to talk about without doing calculations.
- all measuring equipment has error associated with the measurements generated.
- equipment with a graduated scale: error is +- half the smallest scale division. e.g. burette readings are +- 0.05 mL
- percentage error = error on reading/reading x 100%
